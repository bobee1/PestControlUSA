Why pest control in Washington, DC is different

Washington is a dense, old city. Many row houses date to the late 1800s and early 1900s, with shared walls, brick foundations and alleys where trash and recycling attract Norway rats. Construction, sewer work and restaurant waste keep rodent populations active year-round, and the city runs its own rodent control program for public spaces.

Apartment buildings, group houses and a steady flow of visitors make bed bugs a recurring issue, while German cockroaches spread through shared plumbing in multi-unit buildings. Humid summers also bring mosquitoes, including Asian tiger mosquitoes that breed in planters, gutters and small containers in back yards and rooftop gardens.

Rodent control in DC: what the city does and what owners do

DC Health's Rodent and Vector Control Division runs the District's rodent program. Residents report rats through 311, and inspectors survey the area, bait burrows on public property and work with other agencies to enforce trash and sanitation rules. In some cases, such as a neighborhood petition, the program will also treat private yards. Because most rat activity in the District traces back to garbage, the city's efforts focus on how trash is stored, from lidded supercans to sealed compactors for restaurants.

City service has limits. The program does not handle commercial property, and it doesn't trap rats inside homes, seal entry points or treat basements and crawlspaces. Those jobs fall to owners, landlords and businesses, which is where a licensed private provider comes in. The District's rat population is believed to have grown with warmer winters, so many row house owners on Capitol Hill, in Petworth and in Columbia Heights pair 311 reports for the alley with private exclusion work on the house itself.

Pest control tips for Washington, DC residents

  • Use rat-resistant trash cans with tight lids and keep alley areas free of food waste.
  • Report rodent burrows in public spaces to the city's 311 service.
  • In apartments, ask building management to treat adjoining units when roaches or bed bugs appear.
  • Seal gaps around utility lines and under doors with metal mesh or weather stripping, since rats can squeeze through a hole the size of a quarter.
  • Empty saucers, buckets and clogged gutters on patios and roof decks weekly in summer to cut Asian tiger mosquitoes.

Pest control licensing in Washington, DC

Pesticide applicators in the District are certified and regulated by the Department of Energy and Environment, which also limits certain pesticide uses on public and child-occupied properties. Rodent complaints about public spaces go through the city's rodent control program. Hire a certified applicator and request the products used in writing.

Termites and row houses in Washington

Brick row houses in neighborhoods like Capitol Hill, Shaw, Bloomingdale and Petworth still have a lot of wood inside, including floor joists, sills, stair stringers and rear porches. Eastern subterranean termites are common across the region, and they reach that wood through damp basements, cracked foundation walls and wooden back steps that touch soil. Swarms usually appear indoors on warm spring days. Because row houses share walls, a colony found in one home can be active next door too, so neighbors often coordinate inspections. See termite inspection and termite control.

Hiring a pest control company in DC

The District requires every business that advertises or performs pest control in DC to hold a license from the Department of Energy and Environment, and the technicians who apply pesticides must be certified or registered. Many companies that serve the District are based in Maryland or Virginia. They can work in DC, but they need a District license and a resident agent here. DC law also requires approved integrated pest management plans for schools and child-occupied facilities and restricts some pesticide uses near those sites and waterways. Before work starts, ask for the company's DC license and the applicator's ID card.

Pest control in Washington, DC: questions

Who handles rats in Washington, DC?

The city's rodent control program treats public spaces and alleys, often requested through 311. Rats on private property, inside homes or in businesses are the owner's responsibility and are best handled by a licensed pest control provider.
