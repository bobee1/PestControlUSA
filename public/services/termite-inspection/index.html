Termite Inspection & WDI Reports

A termite inspection tells you whether termites or other wood-destroying insects are active, where they have done damage, and what conditions invite them, before you buy, sell or renew a warranty.

Quick answer: A termite inspection is a visual check of the accessible areas of a home for live termites, damage and conditions that attract them. For real estate sales, inspectors complete a wood-destroying insect (WDI) report, often on the NPMA-33 form, which lenders may require. Most homes should be inspected every year.

What's included in termite inspection

  • Exterior foundation, siding, eaves, decks, fences and landscaping
  • Interior baseboards, window and door frames, garage and utility rooms
  • Crawl space piers, sill plates and floor joists, where accessible
  • Attic framing and roof sheathing, where accessible
  • Probing and sounding of suspect wood
  • Moisture meter readings; some inspectors add infrared imaging
  • Notes on conducive conditions such as wood-to-soil contact and leaks
  • A written report with findings, photos where provided, and recommendations

How termite inspection works, step by step

  1. Schedule and prepare. Clear access to the crawl space hatch, attic access, garage walls and under sinks so the inspector can see as much as possible.
  2. Exterior walk. The inspector circles the structure, checking the foundation line, grading, mulch depth, downspouts, decks and any wood touching soil.
  3. Interior and substructure. Rooms, the garage, crawl space and attic are checked for tubes, frass, damage and moisture.
  4. Probe and test. Suspect wood is sounded or probed; moisture meters confirm damp areas; some inspectors use thermal cameras.
  5. Report findings. You receive a written report or WDI form listing evidence of activity, damage, prior treatment and conducive conditions.
  6. Recommend next steps. If termites are active, the report recommends termite treatment; damage repair goes to a contractor.

WDI reports and the NPMA-33 form

In many states, real estate transactions use the Wood Destroying Insect Inspection Report developed by the National Pest Management Association, known as the NPMA-33. It records whether visible evidence of wood-destroying insects was found, whether damage was observed, whether there is evidence of prior treatment, and which areas were inaccessible. Some states use their own official form instead, such as Florida's wood-destroying organisms report and California's Structural Pest Control Board report. Lenders, including some government-backed loan programs in areas with high termite risk, may require a clean report or proof of treatment before closing.

What an inspection can and cannot find

A termite inspection is visual and non-invasive. Inspectors do not remove drywall, flooring, insulation or stored items, so hidden damage behind finished walls can be missed. Reports list inaccessible areas for that reason. If you are buying a home with finished basements, stuffed garages or blocked crawl spaces, ask whether a second visit after clearing access is possible, and read the limitations section of the report carefully.

Other wood-destroying insects an inspection covers

A WDI inspection also looks for carpenter ants, which hollow out damp wood without eating it, carpenter bees, which bore round holes in exposed softwood, and wood-boring beetles such as powderpost beetles, which leave fine flour-like dust and tiny round exit holes. Wood-decay fungi may be noted too, since rot and termite risk go together.

What affects the cost of termite inspection

  • Purpose: an annual homeowner check or a real estate WDI report
  • Size of the home and the number of outbuildings to include
  • Foundation type and how difficult the crawl space or attic is to access
  • Report turnaround needed for a closing date
  • Whether the inspection is bundled with a treatment plan or warranty renewal

Before any termite inspection work begins, get a written estimate covering purpose: an annual homeowner check or a real estate WDI report, the method, visits and guarantee.

DIY vs. calling a professional

Homeowners can do a useful monthly check: walk the foundation looking for mud tubes, check windowsills for wings each spring, keep mulch pulled back and fix leaks. A DIY check cannot produce a WDI report for a sale, and untrained eyes often miss early tubes in crawl spaces. Book a licensed inspection every year, and before buying any home.

Licensing and legal notes

Only licensed operators can issue official WDI reports in most states, and some states require the report on a state-specific form with license numbers. Sellers in several states must disclose known termite damage or treatment. A pest control inspection does not replace a general home inspection or a structural engineer's review of serious damage.

Local note: Lenders and buyers ask for termite inspections most often in the Southeast, Gulf Coast, Southern California and Hawaii, where termite pressure is heaviest. Termite Inspection questions

How long does a termite inspection take?

For a typical single-family home, often about an hour, longer for large homes, multiple outbuildings or difficult crawl spaces.

How often should I get a termite inspection?

Once a year is the common recommendation, and most termite warranties require an annual inspection to stay in force.

Who pays for the termite inspection when buying a home?

It depends on local custom and the purchase contract. Buyers often order it, but in some areas the seller or the loan program determines who pays.

What happens if the inspection finds termites?

The report will recommend treatment. Buyers can negotiate for the seller to treat and repair, and many contracts require proof of treatment before closing.

Does a termite inspection look for other pests?

A WDI inspection covers termites, carpenter ants, carpenter bees and wood-boring beetles. It does not cover rodents, cockroaches or bed bugs unless you ask for a general pest inspection.
