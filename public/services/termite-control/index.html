Termite Control & Termite Treatment

Termites eat wood from the inside out, so most homeowners find them late. A licensed termite exterminator identifies the species and matches the treatment to how that termite lives.

Quick answer: Termite control stops an active colony and protects the structure from new ones. Subterranean termites are usually treated with a liquid termiticide barrier in the soil or with bait stations, while drywood termites live inside the wood and need localized wood treatment or whole-structure fumigation. The EPA notes termites cause billions of dollars in damage every year.

What's included in termite control

  • A full inspection to confirm termites and identify the species
  • A treatment plan and graph showing where products or stations go
  • Liquid termiticide trenching, rodding or slab drilling (subterranean)
  • In-ground bait station installation and monitoring (subterranean)
  • Borate or foam wood treatments for localized activity
  • Tent or tarp fumigation referrals for widespread drywood infestations
  • Moisture and wood-to-soil contact corrections to reduce risk
  • A written warranty or renewable termite bond, when offered

How termite control works, step by step

  1. Inspect and identify. The inspector checks the foundation, crawl space, garage, attic and exterior, probes suspect wood and uses moisture meters to confirm activity and species.
  2. Choose the method. Subterranean colonies get a soil barrier, bait or both. Drywood colonies get spot treatment if contained or fumigation if spread across the structure.
  3. Apply the treatment. Liquid termiticide is placed in a continuous zone around and under the foundation, or bait stations are set at intervals in the soil.
  4. Treat accessible wood. Borate solutions or foams are injected into galleries and applied to exposed framing.
  5. Correct conditions. Wood-to-soil contact, leaks, clogged gutters and mulch against siding are flagged so termites have fewer ways back in.
  6. Monitor and renew. Bait stations are checked on a schedule, and most warranties require an annual re-inspection to stay active.

Subterranean, Formosan, drywood and dampwood termites

Termite species are regional, but the groups and control methods are recognized nationwide, as the University of Florida IFAS Extension explains. Eastern subterranean termites are found in nearly every state and cause most damage. Formosan subterranean termites, an invasive species, form very large colonies and are established along the Gulf Coast, in Florida, Hawaii and parts of the Southeast. Drywood termites live in sound, dry wood without soil contact and are common in southern California, Arizona, Florida and other coastal southern areas. Dampwood termites need wet, decaying wood and are most common in the Pacific Northwest and along the coast.

Mississippi State University Extension notes liquid termiticides are the most commonly used method, while bait systems work more slowly but use far less product. Many providers combine both.

Termite warranties and bonds

A termite warranty (often called a termite bond in the South) is a contract that promises re-treatment, and sometimes repair of new damage, if termites return during the term. Read it closely. Retreat-only bonds pay for treatment but not repairs. Repair bonds cover new damage up to a limit, and many exclude damage that existed before the contract. Most require a paid annual inspection to renew and are transferable to a buyer when you sell the home, which buyers and lenders often value.

Preventing termites after treatment

  • Keep soil, mulch and firewood at least several inches below siding and away from the foundation
  • Fix leaks, slope soil away from the slab, and keep gutters and downspouts draining away from the house
  • Eliminate wood-to-soil contact on decks, fences and porch steps
  • Ventilate crawl spaces and repair damaged vapor barriers
  • Schedule an annual termite inspection so new activity is caught early

What affects the cost of termite control

  • Termite species and how far the infestation has spread
  • Linear feet of foundation and the type: slab, crawl space or basement
  • Method: liquid barrier, bait stations, wood treatment or fumigation
  • Concrete drilling needed for slabs, patios and garages
  • Warranty type (retreat-only or repair) and the annual renewal fee
  • Wood repairs, which are usually separate from the treatment price

Before any termite control work begins, get a written estimate covering termite species and how far the infestation has spread, the method, visits and guarantee.

DIY vs. calling a professional

Retail termite products are not a substitute for a professional barrier. Effective soil treatment needs a continuous zone under slabs and along footings that requires specialized equipment, large product volumes and a license in most states. Homeowners can reduce risk by fixing moisture and wood-to-soil contact, but any sign of mud tubes, swarmers or frass calls for a licensed termite inspection.

Licensing and legal notes

Termite work is a licensed category in every state. Many states, including Florida, Louisiana, Texas and California, require specific record keeping, contracts or notices for wood-destroying organism work, and some require a waiting period or disclosure before fumigation. Verify the company's termite license category with your state pest control regulator.

Termite Control questions

How do I know if I need termite treatment?

Mud tubes on the foundation, swarmers or discarded wings indoors, frass pellets, or wood that sounds hollow are the most reliable signs. A licensed inspection confirms whether termites are active and which species they are.

How long does termite treatment last?

Liquid termiticide barriers are generally designed to protect for several years, depending on the product, soil and disturbance. Bait systems protect as long as stations are monitored and maintained. Most warranties renew annually.

Do I need to leave my home for termite treatment?

Not for liquid or bait treatments. You do need to leave, along with pets and plants, for structural fumigation until the building has been aerated and cleared for re-entry.

Is termite damage covered by homeowners insurance?

Usually not. Most policies treat termite damage as preventable maintenance. A repair-type termite warranty is often the only coverage for new damage.

What's the difference between termites and flying ants?

Termite swarmers have straight antennae, a thick waist and two pairs of equal-length wings. Flying ants have elbowed antennae, a pinched waist and front wings longer than the back pair.

Can termites come back after treatment?

Yes. Treatments protect the structure, but new colonies can forage in. That is why annual inspections and a renewable warranty are standard.
