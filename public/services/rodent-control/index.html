Rodent Control: Mouse & Rat Removal

Scratching in the walls, droppings in the pantry or gnawed wires in the attic mean mice or rats have found a way in. Rodent control removes the ones inside and seals the gaps they used.

Quick answer: Professional rodent control follows the CDC's approach: seal up entry points, trap up the rodents inside, and clean up food and nesting areas. A house mouse can squeeze through a gap about the width of a pencil, so sealing is what keeps a home rodent-free after the trapping ends.

What's included in rodent control

  • Inspection of the attic, crawl space, garage, kitchen and exterior for droppings, rub marks and entry points
  • Species identification: house mouse, deer mouse, Norway rat or roof rat
  • Snap traps and other mechanical traps placed along runways
  • Tamper-resistant, locked bait stations outside where appropriate
  • Exclusion: sealing gaps with steel wool, copper mesh, hardware cloth and sealant
  • Vent, soffit and roof-line screening where rodents climb in
  • Follow-up visits to check traps and remove captured rodents
  • Sanitation advice and cleanup referrals for heavy contamination

How rodent control works, step by step

  1. Inspect. Find droppings, runways and nesting areas, and identify the species, since roof rats, Norway rats and mice need different trap placement.
  2. Map entry points. Check the foundation, utility penetrations, garage door seals, vents, eaves and roof lines for gaps.
  3. Trap inside. Set snap traps perpendicular to walls along runways; avoid poison baits inside the living space where rodents can die in walls.
  4. Control outside. Use locked, tamper-resistant bait stations around the exterior when the label and situation allow.
  5. Seal up. Close every gap with rodent-proof materials once the population inside is down.
  6. Follow up and clean. Check traps until there is no new activity, then clean contaminated areas following CDC guidance.

Mice vs. Norway rats vs. roof rats

The house mouse is the most common rodent inside homes nationwide; it nests close to food and explores new objects readily, so traps work quickly. Norway rats (brown rats) burrow under slabs, sheds and woodpiles and usually enter at ground level. Roof rats, common in southern and coastal states, climb trees, fences and utility lines and nest in attics; they are cautious of new objects, so traps may need to sit unset for a few days. Deer mice in rural and western areas can carry hantavirus, which makes safe cleanup especially important.

Exclusion: the part that keeps rodents out

The CDC recommends looking for gaps between the foundation and the ground, around attic and crawl space vents, and around holes for electrical, plumbing, cable and gas lines, then filling small holes with steel wool held in place with caulk or spray foam. Larger openings call for hardware cloth, sheet metal or cement. Professional exclusion adds door sweeps, garage door seals, vent screens, chimney caps and roof-line repairs, and is often priced separately from trapping.

Health risks and safe cleanup

Rodents contaminate food and surfaces with urine and droppings and can spread diseases including hantavirus, leptospirosis and salmonellosis. Do not sweep or vacuum dry droppings. Following CDC cleanup steps, ventilate the area, wear rubber or plastic gloves, spray droppings and nesting material with a disinfectant or bleach solution, let it soak, then wipe up with paper towels and bag the waste. Heavily contaminated attics may need insulation removal by a cleanup contractor.

Why rodenticide alone is not enough

Poison baits kill rodents slowly and can leave them dying inside walls, creating odors and attracting flies. Since federal rules for consumer rodenticides took effect, retail bait must be sold in tamper-resistant stations to protect children and pets, and many second-generation anticoagulants are restricted to professionals. Pets and wildlife that eat poisoned rodents can be harmed too. Trapping inside plus exclusion is the more reliable long-term fix.

What affects the cost of rodent control

  • Species and population size
  • Number of entry points and how hard they are to reach, such as roof lines
  • Trapping visits needed until activity stops
  • Exclusion materials and labor, often quoted separately
  • Attic cleanup, insulation replacement or sanitizing, if needed
  • Ongoing exterior bait station service for properties with outdoor pressure

Before any rodent control work begins, get a written estimate covering species and population size, the method, visits and guarantee.

DIY vs. calling a professional

A single mouse in a garage can be handled with a few snap traps set along the wall and a check for gaps under the door. Call a professional when you see droppings in more than one room, hear activity in walls or the attic, find rats of any kind, see gnawed wiring, or have trapped for two weeks without the activity stopping. Rats in particular learn to avoid traps and usually point to a structural entry point you have not found.

Licensing and legal notes

Rodenticide application is regulated, and some active ingredients may only be used by certified applicators. Several states and cities restrict certain anticoagulant rodenticides to protect wildlife. Landlords in many jurisdictions are responsible for rodent problems that affect habitability. Check your state and city housing codes if you rent.

Local note: Roof rats are a bigger issue in the Southeast, Gulf Coast, California and the desert Southwest, while Norway rats dominate older Northeast and Midwest cities. Mouse calls spike everywhere in fall as temperatures drop. See rodent pressure by state.

Rodent Control questions

How do I know if I have mice or rats?

Droppings are the best clue. Mouse droppings are about the size of a grain of rice; rat droppings are roughly half an inch or longer. Louder noises, larger gnaw marks and greasy rub marks also point to rats.

How long does it take to get rid of mice?

Many mouse problems are under control within one to three weeks of trapping, as long as entry points are sealed. Rat jobs often take longer because rats avoid new objects.

What size hole can a mouse fit through?

A house mouse can squeeze through a gap about a quarter inch wide, roughly the diameter of a pencil. Young rats can fit through openings about half an inch wide.

Is it safe to clean up mouse droppings myself?

Small amounts can be cleaned safely by following CDC steps: ventilate, wear gloves, soak droppings with disinfectant, then wipe and bag them. Never sweep or vacuum dry droppings.

Will rodents come back after exclusion?

Good exclusion prevents most returns, but new gaps can open as homes settle, vents are damaged or landscaping grows against the house. An annual check keeps the seal intact.

Are bait stations safe around pets?

Tamper-resistant stations are designed to keep bait away from children and pets, but secondary poisoning is still possible if a pet eats a poisoned rodent. Tell your provider about pets so they can choose traps instead where needed.
